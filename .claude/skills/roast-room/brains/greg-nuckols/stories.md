# Stories: Greg Nuckols

Built from 50 of his own Stronger By Science articles (2013 to 2026). 15 stories and case studies he reuses, with the lesson he draws.

1. **The Barbalho case (2018 to 2021).** He noticed a 3 mm arm difference with p = 0.002, identical baselines in steroid and natural groups, and all-even muscle thickness values. With colleagues he published a white paper; journals retracted the studies. Lesson: read the numbers, not just the conclusions, and correct yourself if you promoted bad work. (S021, S002)

2. **The retracted volume studies.** Barbalho's 2019 low-volume studies became the backbone of "5 sets is enough". After retraction, proponents quietly kept their positions. "As is often the case, the initial publication of fake research had a much larger impact on people’s beliefs than the eventual retraction of the fake research." (S002)

3. **Morton 2018 and the protein breakpoint.** A non-significant breakpoint at 1.62 g/kg turned "1 g per lb" into "broscience". His reanalysis put the likely breakpoint near 2 g/kg. Lesson: consensus numbers can rest on shaky stats; read the supplements. (S004)

4. **The creatine hair loss myth.** One 2009 study of 16 rugby players over 3 weeks measured serum DHT, a poor proxy. A 2025 trial with 45 people over 12 weeks found no effect on hormones or hair. Lesson: lazy mechanism reading. (S007)

5. **Creatine "trivial" effect (2023).** A meta-analysis labeled ES 0.11 trivial; he showed it means about a third more growth. Lesson: labels lie. (S008)

6. **The BCAA syllogism.** Every premise true (BCAAs raise synthesis, cut breakdown), conclusion false (direct trials show no extra muscle). Lesson: formally valid logic from mechanisms still misleads. (S002)

7. **The 2016 HMB studies.** HMB supposedly beat moderate-dose steroids in trained lifters. He laid out inconsistencies and would not believe them: "I’m not going to cry outright fraud, but I simply can’t believe the results." (S035)

8. **Soviet periodization.** Born of 1950s top-down planning and GAS, not data; Soviet success owed more to talent systems and drugs. Lesson: lore is not evidence. (S031)

9. **The placebo Dianabol study.** Lifters told they were on steroids added about 100 lb to four lifts in four weeks, versus about 22 lb in the prior seven. Lesson: expectancy changes results; assume you have good genes. (S024)

10. **His own records.** Three all-time drug-free raw records; as an untrained 15-year-old he benched 275 and deadlifted 425. Lesson: talent explains his records more than knowledge or effort. "If a perfectly genetically average person (assuming that exists) was twice as smart about training and worked twice as hard as me, I would still lift more." (S048)

11. **Eze.** A friend who could not bench the bar on day one benched 355 five years later and was one of the most muscular drug-free athletes he knew. Lesson: the starting point says little about potential. (S048)

12. **Fifteen-year-old Greg and Supertraining.** He read it cover to cover and fell for every fad; his first five years were all wrong. Lesson: build a knowledge base before chasing factoids. That became his content filter. (S043)

13. **The Self-Correcting Macro Plan spreadsheet (2015).** It worked about 75% of the time and broke on water-weight swings. Years later it became MacroFactor, built with developer partners. Lesson: ship the simple tool, then rebuild it with the right team. (S016)

14. **Stronger By Science, 2017 to 2023.** MASS plus grad school plus the site meant 2.5 jobs; a fixed content schedule made work "good" but not "special"; MacroFactor had him redlining. Lesson: do fewer things well, and hire. Doing it all himself was "arrogance masquerading as humility". (S006)

15. **His high-volume trouble.** His problems with high volume came from too much overhead pressing (an old baseball shoulder) and heavy squats plus deadlifts together, not volume itself. Lesson: swap the exercises that hurt you before you cut the volume. (S002)
