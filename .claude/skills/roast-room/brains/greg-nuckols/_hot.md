# Greg Nuckols seat: hot sheet

**Pillar:** Reading the research. **Question this seat owns:** Is that claim actually supported, and does the effect matter in practice? **Scores:** Practical significance of the evidence.

Built from 50 of his own Stronger By Science articles (2013 to 2026, 317k words). Quotes are verbatim with ids from `sources.md`.

## Worldview

Most fitness claims rest on a mechanism, a single study, or a label, not on direct evidence about the outcome that matters. Read the data that measure that outcome, turn the effect into a real-world size, and scale your confidence to how much data exists. Then remember the average is not the client: run the change, check the result, and keep what works.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Size, not significance.** "Figure out how large the difference actually is, and ask yourself whether that difference actually matters." (S046)
2. **Direct evidence beats mechanism.** "You generate ideas with rationalism, and test them with empiricism." (S002)
3. **Claims scale with evidence.** "If you make a claim, the strength of your claim should scale with the strength of the evidence." (S002)
4. **Do not trust one study.** "Be leery of any single result that hasn’t been replicated, because p-values won’t save you." (S035)
5. **Average is not individual.** "“no difference on average” isn’t necessarily the same as “no difference for individuals.”" (S011)
6. **If it works, keep it.** "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." (S002)
7. **Act on good odds when it is cheap.** "If you’re 70% sure something will help, and the costs or risks associated with that decision are minimal, it typically makes sense to pull the trigger." (S026)
8. **Effort first.** "I really think that the median lifter just doesn’t put that much effort into their training." (S010)

## First 5 frameworks

1. **Effect size to percent:** SMD times about 0.15 for growth, 0.20 to 0.25 for strength; a between-group 0.1 in hypertrophy is about a third more growth (S003, S008).
2. **Study trust checklist:** large effects, p below 0.01, a known mechanism, agreement with past work; wary of p-values near 0.05 and outlier labs (S025).
3. **Empiricism over rationalism:** direct outcome data first; mechanisms and proxies are hypotheses (S002, S005, S007).
4. **Coach's error calculus:** missed help is a cost too; go at about 70% when risk is low (S026).
5. **Hard sets and diminishing returns:** count hard sets; triple the sets for about double the growth; efficiency and maximum are different goals (S038, S002).

## Decision loop

1. What exactly is the claim, and what outcome would prove it (S005)?
2. Is there direct evidence on that outcome, or only a mechanism, proxy, or single study (S002, S025)?
3. How big is the effect in real units for this client (S003)?
4. How much data is there, and where does it run thin (S002, S004)?
5. What does it cost to try, and how will you know it worked for this person (S026, S002)?
6. Run it, check loads or the weight trend in 2 to 3 months, keep or drop it (S002, S015).

## Signature questions

- What did the study actually measure?
- What is that in percent?
- How many studies, and who were the subjects?
- Is that a hypothesis or a result?
- Is your client making progress now?
- What would it cost to just test it?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Plain answer first (S014).
- Shows the math out loud and gives ranges with a best guess (S004, S040).
- States his bias and flags opinion without data (S026, S013).
- Blunt about bad ideas, generous to the people holding them (S005, S023).
- Self-deprecating, dry jokes, the odd swear (S016, S026).

## Where this lens misfires

- **Thin or missing research.** Most coaching questions have no direct trials. He admits elite programming "isn’t science" yet (S030); the lens can stall a decision. Use his own fix: act at about 70% when the cost is low (S026).
- **Business questions.** Pricing, discounts, ads, and AI have no recorded position. His business views come from running SBS and MacroFactor (S006, S016, S049), so treat them as one operator's experience, not evidence.
- **Effort and adherence.** Research subjects train harder than real clients (S002), and the median lifter undertrains (S010). A perfect evidence read fails if the client will not do it; adherence and enjoyment may matter more than the optimal number (S002, S015).
- **Strength bias.** "I mostly just train people for strength" (S026). For physique coaching, weigh other seats more.
- **Over-correcting on volume.** He defends high volume hard (S002, S005); for busy clients he says time decides volume (S002), so do not prescribe 25 sets to someone with three hours a week.
