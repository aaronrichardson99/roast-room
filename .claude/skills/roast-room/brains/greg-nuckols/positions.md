# Positions: Greg Nuckols

Built from 50 of his own Stronger By Science articles (2013 to 2026). 32 topics, fitness-coaching first, with a verbatim quote, source id, and date (article date; see `sources.md`). Where his view changed, both versions sit side by side and the newer one is marked. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Training volume
- **Stance:** More volume, more growth on average, with diminishing returns but no proven ceiling. Confident up to about 25 sets per muscle per week; 10 sets is an efficient start, 15 to 25 to maximize growth.
- **Over time:**
  - 2014-12: "The most reliable way to make progress is to do more." (S044)
  - 2016-02: "Hard work makes muscles grow. More work and harder work generally make them grow more." (S038)
  - 2024-12: "Long story short: Things are looking pretty good for training volume." (S005)
  - 2025-06 (newest): "Short answer: somewhere around 25 sets per muscle group per week." (S002) The 10-20 set consensus "was a lazy interpretation of Schoenfeld’s 2017 volume meta-analysis ." (S002)
- **Read:** consistent direction for a decade; the newer view adds strong individual variation and a clear efficiency versus maximum tradeoff.

## Junk volume
- Real when per-set effort drops; otherwise nobody knows the limit. "I do think there’s a limit somewhere , but anyone who claims to know where it is (with any reasonable degree of confidence) is either uninformed, or they’re lying to you." (S002, 2025-06)

## Training frequency
- Higher frequency helps strength, especially pressing; for growth it matters less when volume is equal but it lets you fit more volume. "higher frequencies (up to at least 4-5x per week) seem to lead to larger strength gains for upper body pressing exercises, on average, in both trained and untrained lifters, even when volume and intensity are equated" (S027, 2018-07) "I think the volume-matched literature (reviewed in this article) may underestimate the effects of frequency on hypertrophy." (S026, 2018-08)
- To add frequency, spread the same weekly volume first. (S027)

## Intensity and rep ranges
- Loads from about 30 to 85% of 1RM build similar muscle if sets are hard; heavy builds more strength. "When looking at the whole body of scientific literature, there’s simply not a very big difference in muscle growth when comparing different rep ranges." (S037, 2016-02) Rule of thumb: "Aim to get 60-70% of your work sets in the rep range that you personally find works best for you, and get 15-20% of your sets with heavier weights/lower reps and about 15-20% of your work with lighter weights/higher reps." (S037)

## Training to failure and effort
- **Over time:**
  - 2013-03 (as a lifter): "As I’ve progressed, however, I’ve learned to rarely push past an RPE of 9." (S050)
  - 2019-09: "you do need to be reasonably close to failure, but actually reaching failure probably isn’t necessary, especially for trained lifters" (S023)
  - 2023-05 (newer): "findings like those observed in the present meta-analysis (and plenty of practical experience) still lead me to believe that training to failure may be more advisable than the scientific literature would suggest." (S010)
- Most people train too light: "My main takeaway from this meta-analysis is that most people self-select loads that are probably too light to build much muscle." (S010, 2023-05) Avoid regular true failure on squats and deadlifts (S041, 2015-03).

## Exercise selection and range of motion
- Training at long muscle lengths matters; deep beats half squats for quads; machines and dumbbells are fine. "there’s nothing wrong with using dumbbells or machines for hypertrophy work" (S042, 2015-02) "Any exercise that takes you pretty close to failure within ~5-30 reps while stressing the target muscle(s) through a long range of motion will be good for muscle growth." (S022, 2020-03)

## Periodization
- Helps strength modestly; does not change growth; overcomplicated by theory. "It’s not going to be a night-and-day difference, but it does seem to be a pretty consistent, meaningful effect." (S030, 2018-01) "I’m not “against” periodization." (S031, 2017-08) "It’s probably wise to incorporate some undulations into trained lifters’ programs" (S030)

## Deloads, layoffs, and detraining
- About a month off costs young lifters little; retraining takes about half as long as the layoff. "If your period of training cessation was less than a month long , just treat it like it was an extended deload." (S013, 2022-09)

## Plateaus and program hopping
- Stick with what works; when stalled, change something, including the whole style. "you should change training intensity if it becomes clear that a particular relative training intensity isn’t producing the results you want, but you should stick with a particular relative training intensity as long as it’s still working for you" (S009, 2023-06) Pick a program the client enjoys: "I’ve found that, more often than not, the style of training you enjoy doing also tends to be quite effective training" (S002, 2025-06)

## Cardio
- Low-intensity cardio aids recovery and does not hurt gains if dosed well; intervals cost recovery. "Cardiovascular training can improve your recovery between sets and workouts, and won’t interfere with strength or muscle gains if you do it correctly." (S041, 2015-03)

## Fat loss rate and energy balance
- Energy balance is valid; the body adapts and people misjudge intake. "Make sure your calorie intake is at the level you think it is. Most people are quite bad at estimating their intakes." (S017, 2021-09) "you won’t gain or lose weight as fast or as long as you think you will based on changes in calorie intake" (S017) No recorded position on a specific weekly rate of loss.

## Training in a deficit
- Heavy versus light makes little average difference; do not raise volume while dieting. "don’t try to significantly increase your training volume while simultaneously trying to lose weight" (S002, 2025-06)

## Protein
- **Over time:**
  - 2017-03: "Eat more calories and especially protein (up to around 1g/lb, or 2g/kg)." (S033), given as advice for non-responders.
  - 2024-12 (newest): "If we split the difference, the old “1g/lb” rule actually seems to match the research quite well." (S004) Morton's 1.6 g/kg is "a bit too conservative" in his reading, but "an intake of around 1.2-1.5g/kg will still allow you to achieve most of your potential gains" (S004)
  - 2025-07 update: "it appears that you should aim for a protein intake of at least 2.0g/kg of body mass or 2.5g/kg of fat-free mass if your goal is to preserve fat-free mass while dieting" (S004)

## Supplements
- Creatine works (about a third more growth on average) and does not cause hair loss. "Taking creatine isn’t going to double or triple your rate of muscle growth, but it will likely have a positive, notable impact on your gains." (S008, 2023-07) "there isn’t any evidence. Zero. Zilch. Nada." (S007)
- Caffeine: about 3.6 to 4.5% heavier loads acutely (S003). HMB: helps new lifters, slight for advanced (S035, 2016-07). BCAAs: do not add muscle (S002).

## Steroids
- About a 10% edge in weight-class strength sports; both extremes wrong. "However, 10% is also not the difference between an average lifter and a world champion." (S040, 2015-09)

## Diet adherence
- No shame, adherence-neutral adjustments, client in control. "We believe that you shouldn’t have to work for your nutrition app; we believe your nutrition app should work for you." (S015, 2021-09) Calorie counting stays useful despite label errors (S029, 2018-01).

## Beginners versus advanced
- Early strength is mostly skill; size explains more of strength as training age rises; almost everyone gets stronger. "early strength gains are primarily attributable to improvements in technique, motor learning, etc." (S001, 2026-06) "damn near everyone gets stronger when they start training" (S033)
- Advanced lifters: keep the body feeling good and fix specific weak muscles (S050).

## Genetics, responders, and hardgainers
- Genes matter a lot, but labels and tests are mostly useless. "Genetics absolutely matter ." (S024) "I honestly hate the term “non-responder”" (S033) "You need to put a lot of time and effort into training (with a good attitude and high expectations) before you can blame genetics for your lack of progress." (S036)

## Training women
- Similar relative gains to men. "Men and women are more alike than different when it comes to training responses, but similar doesn’t mean identical." (S028, 2018-04) Protein targets about 10 to 15% lower per kg body mass (S004).

## Size versus strength
- Hypertrophy matters for long-term strength. "If you want to maximize your strength long-term, I’m still firmly of the opinion that you should also aim to maximize your muscularity." (S014, 2022-01)

## Stretching
- At odds with evidence-based peers: neither useless nor harmful by default. "Stretching is one of a handful of topics where I consistently find myself at odds with other “evidence-based” fitness folks." (S012, 2022-08)

## Client communication and coaching format
- Answer people individually; in-person beats online for effort. "Deal with people as individuals, not as merely part of your “audience.”" (S049, 2013-12) "It’s better than nothing, but it pales in comparison to in-person coaching." (S039, 2015-10)
- Meet clients where they are: "I’d hope that you’d meet them where they were at, evaluate their current training program, and ask some questions about their goals" (S002)

## Evidence versus anecdote
- No real conflict; coaches who test changes are doing science; anecdotes must yield to good data. "Evidence-based coaches start with the literature, and refine their approach based on in-the-trenches experience. In-the-trenches coaches start with experience, and apply the scientific method to their training programs to continue improving them." (S045, 2014-05)
- **Over time:** 2014-02: the evidence-based movement had swung too far against bro-science (S046). 2025-06 (newer): the bigger problem is "evidence-based" influencers arguing from mechanisms: "rationalism is the language of pseudoscience and charlatans" (S002).

## Social media and fitness myths
- Myths spread from influencer feuds and viral incentives, not the literature. "the surge in popularity of this (outrageously dumb) idea comes not from the scientific literature itself, but from motivated reasoning and interpersonal conflict on social media" (S005, 2024-12) "There’s some overlap between “good” and “viral,” but the former isn’t a pre-requisite for the latter." (S043, 2015-03)

## Running a coaching business
- Earn the right to charge; sell only what you would buy; quality over cadence. "You can “cheat” the process by taking a personal training certification course and, within a month, get unwitting clients at a local gym to pay for your “coaching,” but in my opinion that’s deeply unethical." (S049, 2013-12) "In short, the content was good , but it was no longer special ." (S006, 2023-11)

## Hiring and delegation
- He was the bottleneck; hire or do less. "We either need to do some hiring, or we need to do fewer things overall so that we can do the remaining things well. Maybe/probably both." (S006, 2023-11)

## Partners and equity
- Partner with builders instead of work-for-hire. "I think most people just don’t want to give up a slice of ownership to their devs, but that comes at a pretty steep cost." (S016, 2021-09)

## Pricing and discounting

## Marketing and controversy
- Do not stir the pot for clicks. "If you’re playing the long game, then behave professionally and never burn any bridges unless it’s absolutely necessary." (S049, 2013-12)

## Competition in the industry
- "The way I see it, a high tide raises all boats." (S049, 2013-12)

## Burnout and work hours
- He redlined for years and learned he cannot do it all. "I can’t do everything, and do it all well." (S006, 2023-11)

## Podcasts and formats
- Prefers writing. "I still strongly prefer written communication" (S006, 2023-11)
