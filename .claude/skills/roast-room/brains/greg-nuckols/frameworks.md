# Frameworks: Greg Nuckols

Built from 50 of his own Stronger By Science articles (2013 to 2026, bylined Greg Nuckols), read through 14 notes batches. 18 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources that use the idea. Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The "How to score" lines are scoring guidance for the council, built from his own thresholds. They are not quotes.

---

## 1. Effect size to real-world percent *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Turn every standardized effect size into a percent change people can feel before judging it. Multiply the SMD by the typical coefficient of variation: about 0.15 for hypertrophy, 0.20 to 0.25 for strength. A typical study moves muscle size about 5% (ES about 0.34) and strength about 22% (ES about 0.87).
- **Parts:** Find the SMD; pick the CV for the outcome; get the additive percent; divide by the typical control gain to get the multiplicative difference; ignore the verbal label.
- **When to use:** Any claim built on a meta-analysis: a supplement, a rep range, a protein target, a program tweak.
- **How to score:** Guidance: between-condition ES 0.1 in hypertrophy is about a third more growth (not trivial); ES 0.3 in hypertrophy is nearly double; ES 0.3 in strength is about 20 to 40% bigger gains. Score the claim on the percent, not on "small" or "trivial".
- **Quote:** "if you’d like to roughly estimate the relative effect of some hypertrophy intervention where a meta-analysis reports an SMD effect size, you can just multiply the effect size by 0.15" (S003)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S008, S005)

## 2. Significance is not relevance *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A statistically significant difference can be too small to matter, and a "trivial" label can hide a real effect. Find the size of the difference, then ask whether it matters for this person.
- **When to use:** Whenever someone cites "a study showed" or "significant".
- **How to score:** Guidance: 30 versus 31 lb lost in groups of 1,000 is significant and meaningless; low.
- **Quote:** "Don’t be misled when people are claiming a “significant” finding. Figure out how large the difference actually is, and ask yourself whether that difference actually matters." (S046)
- **Sources:** 4 (S046, S003, S008, S025)

## 3. Study trust checklist *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Why published results go wrong (publication bias, p-hacking, sloppiness, HARKing, low power) and what raises trust: biological plausibility, agreement with past research, funding, p-values well below 0.05, large effects, pre-registration, how hard the study was to run, expert prediction, objective measures.
- **When to use:** A single new study is driving a decision.
- **How to score:** Guidance: raise trust for large effects, p below 0.01, a known mechanism, and agreement with prior work. Lower it for many p-values between 0.04 and 0.05, a result far from similar studies, or industry funding from a lab with eye-popping results.
- **Quote:** "The simplest and most straightforward things to look for are large effects, small p-values (below 0.01, not 0.05), results that can be explained by known mechanisms, and results that jive with prior research." (S025)
- **Sources:** 5 (S025, S035, S021, S004, S019)

## 4. Empiricism over rationalism (direct evidence beats mechanism)
- **What it is:** Data that measure the outcome you care about beat logical chains built from mechanisms. Logic makes hypotheses; data test them. A plausible mechanism is weak evidence: more than 99% of mechanistically promising drugs fail in trials.
- **Parts:** Ask what direct evidence exists on this outcome; treat mechanism and proxy outcomes (strength for size, serum DHT for hair loss, MPS for growth) as hypothesis; scale claim strength to the evidence.
- **When to use:** A coach, influencer, or client argues from "how it should work".
- **How to score:** Guidance: a large body of direct longitudinal data rates high; a single proxy study or a mechanism story rates low however tidy it sounds.
- **Quote:** "You generate ideas with rationalism, and test them with empiricism." (S002)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S005, S007, S012, S014, S020, S025)

## 5. Claim strength scales with evidence *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Lots of direct evidence allows strong claims; a little allows tentative ones; none means you say it is speculation. He applies it to himself: confident on volume up to about 25 sets because data density drops after that.
- **Quote:** "If you make a claim, the strength of your claim should scale with the strength of the evidence." (S002)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S004, S013, S040, S041)

## 6. The coach's error calculus (Type I versus Type II) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Scientists guard against false positives. Coaches face low stakes and should count missed help as a cost too. Act at about 70% confidence when the downside is small and reversible.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to try a change with a client before the research is settled.
- **How to score:** Guidance: low cost, reversible, about 70% sure it helps, go. High cost or irreversible, demand more.
- **Quote:** "If you’re 70% sure something will help, and the costs or risks associated with that decision are minimal, it typically makes sense to pull the trigger." (S026)
- **Sources:** 2 (S026, S046)

## 7. Average is not the individual *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Group means hide responders. Tripling volume from 6 to 18 sets gave only about a 2 in 5 chance of meaningfully more growth; roughly a quarter of people respond dramatically to high volume, a quarter somewhat, a quarter not at all, a quarter better with less. Turn individual data into odds.
- **Quote:** "We should also remember that “no difference on average” isn’t necessarily the same as “no difference for individuals.”" (S011)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S008, S009, S011, S019, S027, S033)

## 8. If it is working, do not change it; when it stalls, troubleshoot *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His recurring decision tree. Progressing: change nothing. Stalled and not worn down: do more, change one variable, or try a style that worked before. Cannot recover from more: build work capacity first.
- **Quote:** "Essentially, if you’re getting stronger, don’t fix what isn’t broken. If you’re not getting stronger, assuming you aren’t feeling worn down all the time, do more." (S044)
- **Sources:** 6 (S044, S002, S026, S009, S046, S033)

## 9. Hard sets as the volume metric
- **What it is:** Count sets taken within a couple reps of failure. Volume load misleads across exercises, loads, and ranges of motion.
- **Quote:** "there’s the way I personally like to measure training volume: number of hard sets , or sets within a couple reps of failure" (S038)
- **Sources:** 3 (S038, S023, S002)

## 10. Diminishing returns are not lower returns
- **What it is:** Volume and hypertrophy scale roughly 3:2 (triple the sets, about double the growth). Efficiency and maximum results are different goals. Pick one and say which.
- **How to score:** Guidance: 10 sets per week is a good efficient start; 15 to 25 to maximize growth; confident up to about 25.
- **Quote:** "Diminishing returns aren’t lower total returns." (S002)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S044)

## 11. Four ways to raise volume
- **What it is:** Least to most recommended: dive in; progressive volume cycling; a gradual ramp of about 20% every 2 to 4 weeks; body part specialization blocks. Check loads after 2 to 3 months: higher means it worked, lower means try something else.
- **Quote:** "The main reason I tend to favor body part specialization blocks is that they let you take a test drive of high volumes without having to fully commit to the strategy." (S002)
- **Sources:** 1 (S002)

## 12. Within-study slopes (the Simpson's Paradox check) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Plotting outcome against dose across studies mixes up subject differences (trained lifters test higher protein and grow less). Compute the effect per unit dose inside each study instead, and bracket the answer with one analysis biased low and one biased high.
- **Quote:** "I think what we’re dealing with here is an example of Simpson’s Paradox ." (S004)
- **Sources:** 1 (S004)

## 13. Simulate it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before trusting a statistic or a claim, model it. Build dummy data where nothing is related and see what the method returns; simulate 1,000 mislabeled foods; simulate how likely a data pattern is.
- **Quote:** "See what repeated measures r-value functionally equates to r = 0 for your dataset, and keep that in mind when interpreting your results." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S021, S029)

## 14. Hard versus soft version of an idea *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Separate the viral simple version of a claim from the defensible one, and rebut only the first. "Only the last 5 reps matter" fails; "sets need to be hard" is almost self-evident.
- **Quote:** "I think the “hard” version of the idea (“the last 5 reps before failure are all the matters”) has major problems, but a “soft” version of the idea is almost self-evidently true." (S023)
- **Sources:** 2 (S023, S012)

## 15. Compass, not road map
- **What it is:** Research gives principles and direction, not step-by-step programs. Coaches fill the gap with N=1 data, changing one variable at a time.
- **Quote:** "They should be more of a compass than a road map: they point you in the right general direction; they DON’T give you step-by-step instructions." (S046)
- **Sources:** 3 (S046, S023, S047)

## 16. Precision over accuracy *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Calorie labels can be off by 20%, but random errors cancel and consistent errors keep you precise. Precision is what you need to adjust intake.
- **Quote:** "Your calorie counts are wrong, but that wrongness doesn’t limit their utility for knowing what you need to do to gain or lose weight, since they’re all wrong in the same direction." (S029)
- **Sources:** 2 (S029, S017)

## 17. Adherence-neutral, no-shame coaching
- **What it is:** The MacroFactor design: estimate expenditure from logged intake and weight trend, adjust weekly whatever the adherence, and never shame. No shame means more honest logs, which means better recommendations.
- **When to use:** Client communication, check-ins, nutrition coaching.
- **Quote:** "If we were jerks, our algorithms would be analyzing less accurate data from more users, and would therefore be making worse recommendations." (S015)
- **Sources:** 2 (S015, S016)

## 18. Body as garden, not machine
- **What it is:** Life stress draws on the same adaptive reserves as training, so the same program can be easy or crushing. Build wiggle room into plans.
- **Quote:** "Conceptually, you should think of your body not as a machine, but rather as a garden." (S047)
- **Sources:** 2 (S047, S031)
